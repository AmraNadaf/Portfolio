Cutting the middleman out of print fulfilment

in production

Integrated Lulu's print-on-demand service directly into our storefronts across the Indian and US markets, replacing Ingram as the intermediary handling printing, shipping and fulfilment.

Order placed→ Manuscript PDF→ Lambda (Docker) reformat→ Lulu print job→ Direct to customer

The problem

Every printed book went through Ingram, who handled printing, shipping and handling and charged for the privilege. Their cut was roughly half the profit on every book, or more — worth more than any efficiency gain elsewhere in the pipeline.

[[ Pin this down before publishing: half of *profit* or half of *cover price*? Very different claims, and someone will ask. Get the exact figure and the annual volume, then rewrite the sentence above without the hedge. ]]

How it works

Lulu won't accept an arbitrary PDF — print jobs have to arrive in a specific format, so this was never a matter of forwarding an order. I wrote AWS Lambda functions, packaged as Docker images, that take the incoming manuscript PDF and transform it into the spec Lulu's press accepts before the print order is submitted. The integration runs against Amazon, Shopify and Wix storefronts in both marketplaces, and I extended the company's bulk-order dashboard to route bulk orders through the same path.

[[ Two or three more sentences: where job state lives, what happens when Lulu rejects a file, and how a failed print job gets retried or surfaced. ]]

Decisions I'd defend

Container images over Lambda layers. The transformation needs Ghostscript and several other PDF tools, and that dependency set doesn't fit inside a Lambda layer — layers are size-capped and can't be expanded, so building around them would have meant stripping the toolchain down to whatever squeezed in and hoping it held. A container image gives the job room for what it actually needs, and the environment is reproducible rather than assembled from whatever fit.

[[ One more decision: why Lulu over other print-on-demand providers? Even "the only one with an API we could integrate against" is a real answer — the constraint is more interesting than a feature list. ]]

What it changed

Ingram is out of the chain. We deal with the customer directly, and the margin on every printed book improved.

A self-serve audiobook platform for authors

building

A platform, deployed serverless on AWS, that lets authors turn their own poetry books into narrated audiobooks. Built solo, end to end.

Bubble session→ Voice demo→ SQS queue→ Worker × poem→ Music & branding→ Audiobook

What an author does

They hear four voices first — two male, two female — and pick the one their book gets read in. The book then goes into a queue; producing one takes around twenty minutes, so they can wait or close the tab and get notified when it's ready. From there they can re-record up to twice, correct pronunciation, or prompt for a different tone of reading. Last, they choose background music and hear it under their own poems before committing. The finished file opens with the book's title and the author's name, and closes on the company's branding.

The constraint that shaped it

A Lambda invocation is capped at fifteen minutes. A book takes about twenty. So the platform is split in two: an API Lambda that accepts the job and a worker Lambda that does the narration, decoupled by an SQS queue. The book is chunked poem by poem and each poem goes to Google's Gemini API as its own call, so no single invocation ever runs long. The twenty minutes is wall-clock across the queue, not one long process — which is also why an author can close the tab and walk away. Because each poem is its own queued message, a poem that fails retries on its own, up to three times, without restarting the book. Finished audio is written to S3 and served from there.

Decisions I'd defend

The handoff is invisible. The main site runs on Bubble, the audiobook platform runs on AWS, and an author shouldn't have to notice or log in twice. I carry the session across from Bubble using its own authentication, via a session token and cookie, so the author moves between two entirely different stacks without seeing a seam.

The controls are editorial, not technical. Pronunciation fixes, tone prompts and two re-records let an author correct what's actually wrong with a reading without needing to understand anything about the synthesis underneath.

Automated cover and author verification

in production

A computer vision pipeline that reads author-submitted book covers, checks the author's details and the layout against the same list a human reviewer used to work through, and flags anything questionable for review.

Cover upload→ OpenCV preprocess→ EasyOCR extract→ LLM check→ Pass / flag

The problem

Every cover was checked by hand, one book at a time, by an agent on the team. They were looking for a specific list of failures: text sitting too close to the edge, where the trim would cut it off; an award badge overlapping the title; a cover that had come through blank; a wrong title or subtitle; a misspelled author name. All of it caught by eye, on every single book.

[[ Two numbers would finish this: roughly how many covers a day, and how long a manual check took. "Forty a day at five minutes each" is a sentence someone will quote back to you in an interview. ]]

How it works

OpenCV handles image preprocessing, EasyOCR pulls the text off the cover, and an OpenAI model evaluates what was extracted against the author's expected details. It catches the same list the agents were looking for — edge margins, badge collisions, blank covers, wrong titles and subtitles, misspelled author names — and flags anything questionable for a human rather than rejecting it outright.
